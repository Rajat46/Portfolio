Aakar — Building a Design Development System Without Formal Authority

How I diagnosed a design organization problem, built an operating model for growth, and led change before I had the title.

01. The Problem

About a year after joining UST as a Senior Lead Designer, I started seeing warning signs on the Dell account: negative client feedback, inconsistent design quality, and a risk that designers could leave the account entirely.

My manager oversaw 75 people, including 23 designers, but did not have a design background. She relied on me as the most senior designer in the group and asked me to figure out what was actually going wrong.

I did not have formal authority over the 23 designers. I had to solve the problem through credibility, relationships, evidence, and influence.

1.1 What was happening?

The symptoms appeared across three areas.

Client Team Growth
Negative feedback Inconsistent knowledge sharing Workday goals becoming checkbox exercises
Inconsistent design quality Designers solving similar problems independently Development disconnected from actual team needs
Risk to the account Knowledge staying with individuals Designers looking outward for courses instead of inward to the team

The obvious interpretation was:

The team needs to get better at design.

I didn't want to accept that assumption without understanding where the gap actually was.

1.2 The Diagnosis

I reviewed performance ratings and met one-on-one with every designer. The pattern was different from what I expected.

What I looked at What I found
Senior designer performance Senior designers were already rated above 4.0
Where the capability gap was concentrated More heavily among mid and junior designers
Development gaps Structured development, certifications, storytelling, and presenting
Knowledge flow Designers were repeatedly solving problems independently
Existing growth process Workday goals had become disconnected from actual team needs

The data changed the problem. This wasn't simply a design-skill problem. It was a system problem:

Capability wasn't developing consistently
↓
Knowledge wasn't moving across the team
↓
Individual goals weren't connected to collective needs
↓
Contribution and growth were difficult to make visible

That changed the question I needed to solve. Not:

How do we train designers to become better?

But:

How do we create an environment where designers can learn from one another, develop intentionally, and make their contribution visible?

That became the foundation for what I built next.

02. The Leadership Hypothesis

I proposed a program that could connect individual development to team learning, create more opportunities for contribution, and rebuild client confidence from the inside out.

I called it Aakar, a Hindi word meaning "shape."

The idea was simple:

Designers are shaped by how they participate, not only by title or years of experience.

A junior designer who consistently takes initiative can demonstrate leadership before receiving a senior title. In a services environment where teams change frequently, those behaviors are more durable than hierarchy.

I pitched the idea to my manager. She approved it, and I began running it as a Senior Lead Designer rather than as a formal manager. That constraint shaped the design of the program. I couldn't rely on reporting lines to make it work.

03. Designing the Participation Model

Instead of organizing development strictly around seniority, I mapped designers by how they were actually participating. The model identified five broad patterns:

  • Proactive
    Consistently taking initiative, leading discussions, and volunteering for challenges.
  • Active
    Dependable contributors with regular participation and room to stretch into broader leadership.
  • Partially Active
    Contributing when prompted and needing more structure to build confidence and consistency.
  • Interested
    Showing curiosity but not yet participating consistently.
  • New Joiners
    Still adapting to the account, team culture, and ways of working.

The point was not to label people permanently. It was to understand where each person was starting from and create the right opportunities for them to grow.

3.1 The principle underneath the model

Leadership potential should not be reserved for the people who already have the seniority.

Because I had no formal title, I also needed the framework to feel larger than personal preference. I grounded it in UST's servant-leadership philosophy: the role of a leader is to empower others rather than simply direct them.

That gave the program an organizational principle people already recognized rather than making it feel like a peer-created initiative imposed on the team.

04. Turning the Framework Into an Operating System

A framework by itself would not change behavior. I needed a rhythm that made participation repeatable.

4.1 The Aakar learning structure

The program was organized around four learning areas:

  • Circle — Philosophy, Psychology, and History of UX
    The foundations and human context behind the discipline.
  • Triangle — Growth and Movement with AI
    Emerging tools, technology, and forward-looking practice.
  • Rectangle — Canvas of Tools
    The practical tools designers used to execute their work.
  • Square — Design Process and Accessibility
    Rigor, process, and the responsibility to design for everyone.

The visual language made the program memorable. The operating rhythm made it real.

4.2 The cadence

  • Weekly: One designer owned and delivered a presentation every Tuesday.
  • Monthly: Each group contributed four presentations, one per week.
  • Quarterly: Each group worked around a theme, with three weeks of presentations and a fourth week reserved for reflection and feedback.

The recurring loop was:

Explore → Present → Discuss → Refine

I participated in the sessions myself rather than managing them from a distance. The program was not something I asked the team to do for me. I was part of the system.

05. Making It Work Inside the Real Organization

The first version of the system was not perfect. Several constraints became visible only after the program was running.

5.1 Connecting development to Workday

Workday's quarterly goals were pushing designers toward disconnected, checkbox-style development topics. Rather than fight the existing process, I mapped each designer's official goals to their Aakar work. The corporate requirement and the actual development activity became the same path rather than two competing systems.

5.2 Handling resistance

Not everyone wanted the same kind of development structure. Two designers told me they preferred to pursue their own certification plans rather than join the standard Aakar structure.

I didn't force them into it. Instead, I asked them to define SMART goals around their certifications and track progress with evidence using the same accountability principle as Aakar. The objective was not uniform participation. It was structured, visible growth.

Two other designers felt Aakar was additional work on top of their normal responsibilities. This required a harder conversation. Because my manager had given me visibility into their performance ratings, I knew their development gaps were larger than they realized. I explained directly why the additional investment mattered for them specifically. It wasn't a comfortable conversation, but it was a necessary one. Both remained in the program.

5.3 Building the incentive structure

As the program matured, I identified another problem: participation was asking for effort without a clear recognition mechanism. I went back to my manager and proposed connecting Aakar contribution to formal recognition. She agreed, and the program moved to quarterly recognition, including a formal award tied directly to a designer's Aakar contribution.

The system had to make participation worthwhile, not simply possible.

5.4 Getting honest feedback

Because I did not have a reporting line, I couldn't assume that participation meant genuine buy-in. I created an anonymous feedback loop through MS Forms and treated the team culture like a product:

Measure → Learn → Iterate

That gave me a way to see where the program was creating value, where it was creating friction, and what needed to change.

06. What Changed

I wanted the program to be measured by more than participation. Over the following 14 months, the indicators we were trying to improve moved together with the program.

6.1 Client satisfaction: 3.5 → 4.2+ CSAT

Dell CSAT moved from 3.5 to a consistent 4.2+ over the 14-month period. For a services organization, client satisfaction isn't simply an internal culture metric. It is closely connected to the health of the client relationship and, ultimately, the business.

6.2 Designer growth: 2 designers promoted to Senior roles

Aakar didn't create those promotions by itself. What it did was create a more structured environment in which contribution, quality, discipline, and growth became more visible.

6.3 Knowledge became an asset

The team also co-authored a UX course that became the primary onboarding asset for new designers joining the account. Knowledge that had previously lived inside individual designers became something the team could reuse.

6.4 The system lasted

The final test wasn't whether people participated for a few weeks because something was new. It was whether the system continued to work after the novelty disappeared. It operated across the full 14-month period.

07. What This Changed for Me

Aakar gave me evidence that I could lead beyond my own design output. I was still an individual contributor when I started. I had no direct reports and no formal management mandate.

But I had diagnosed an organizational problem, built a system to address it, adapted that system around real constraints, handled difficult conversations, and sustained it long enough to see measurable change.

I used that evidence in a direct conversation with my manager and made an explicit ask:

I wanted to pursue a management path rather than remain a consultant.

That conversation, together with the broader trust I had built across the client and delivery organization, led to my move into formal people management. Today, I manage a design team while continuing to work on the Dell account as a Principal Product Designer.

Reflection

Aakar changed how I think about design leadership. The work was not really about creating a learning program. It was about creating the conditions in which other designers could contribute, grow, and become more visible.

I learned that leading without formal authority requires more than having a good idea. It requires diagnosing the real problem before prescribing a solution, designing structures people can actually participate in, adapting when those structures meet resistance, and staying accountable for whether the system works.

The title came later. The leadership work came first.
